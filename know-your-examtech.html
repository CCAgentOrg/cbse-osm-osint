Know Your ExamTech

Why India’s Exam System Keeps Getting Captured by Small Firms — And What It Means for You

Published: 31 May 2026

If you’re reading this, you probably had a terrible experience with your CBSE results this month. Your answer sheets were scanned with pages missing. Your marks looked blurry. Some students even found their scores swapped with someone else’s. You protested. The CBSE announced a re-evaluation for thousands.

Here’s the thing: what happened to you with CBSE is not unique. The exact same pattern has played out with SSC exams. It’s building right now with NEET. And the government’s own education policy is about to make it worse.

This is a guide to the exam-tech industry in India — the companies that build the systems your exams run on, how they get those contracts, why the big companies keep losing, and why nobody seems to fix it.

1. The Market: Your Marks, Their Business

Every year, India runs an examination ecosystem that processes:

  • ~2 crore candidates taking national exams (NTA alone conducts 15+ exams)
  • ~18.5 lakh CBSE Class 12 answer sheets
  • ~50 lakh SSC exam aspirants
  • ~30+ state boards with their own exam systems
  • ~25 crore school students overall

All of this needs technology: scanning, evaluation, mark processing, result compilation, CBT (computer-based test) delivery, proctoring.

The market for this is worth thousands of crores annually. And it’s served by a handful of companies you’ve never heard of.

Two of these companies — Coempt and Eduquity — have been at the centre of major scandals in the last 12 months. Both followed almost identical playbooks.

2. The SSC Scam: Déjà Vu

While you were dealing with the CBSE OSM mess, another scandal was unfolding at the Staff Selection Commission (SSC) — which conducts exams for government jobs that millions of young Indians prepare years for.

The Eduquity Story

In 2025, the SSC replaced TCS (which had been running SSC exams) with Eduquity Career Technologies, a Bengaluru-based company. Eduquity won the contract by quoting lower than TCS.

What happened next:

  • July 2025: SSC Phase-13 exam — server crashes, biometric failures, students turned away from centres. Chaos across India.
  • Paper leak allegations: Repeated questions, alleged leaks in SSC GD 2026 exam.
  • Massive protests: Students took to the streets demanding Eduquity be removed.
  • YouTuber sued: Eduquity filed a ₹25 crore defamation case against YouTuber Nitish Rajput for exposing the scandal.
  • SSC Chairman’s defence: Chairman S. Gopalakrishnan claimed Eduquity’s role was limited to “conducting examinations, not question paper setting.” 1

The Blacklist That Didn’t Stick

Here’s where it gets wild. Eduquity was blacklisted by the Directorate General of Training (DGT) in 2020 for exam-related irregularities. The DGT’s own comparative statement document lists Eduquity as “Ineligible.” 2

Yet in 2025, Eduquity won the SSC contract anyway.

How? The same way Coempt won the CBSE contract:

The Playbook: Step by Step

Step 1: The Big Firm Qualifies - CBSE OSM: TCS qualified in round 1 and round 2, but no winner emerged - SSC CBT: TCS had been running exams smoothly, but was replaced

Step 2: Tender Conditions Get Relaxed - CBSE: Scanning resolution dropped from 300+ DPI. CMMI Level 5 certification requirement removed. Bidder eligibility criteria watered down. 3 - SSC: QCBS (Quality-Cost Based Selection) criteria weighted to favour lower financial bids over technical capability

Step 3: The Small Firm Wins on Price - CBSE: Coempt quoted ₹24.75 per answer sheet vs TCS’s higher bid 4 - SSC: Eduquity quoted ~₹273 crore for the CBT cycle — winning on lowest financial bid 5

Step 4: The System Fails - CBSE: Missing pages, blurred marks, score swaps - SSC: Server crashes, biometric failures, cancelled exams, paper leak allegations

Step 5: Political Insulation - CBSE: BJP defenders claim “Coempt works in opposition-ruled states too” — implying cross-party legitimacy - SSC: Chairman claims Eduquity’s role was limited, blames “rumours”

Step 6: Students Pay the Price - You. Your friends. Millions of aspirants whose futures depend on these exams.

This is not coincidence. This is a pattern.

3. Why TCS Keeps Losing

You’d think India’s largest IT company would dominate government exam tech. It doesn’t. Here’s why:

A. The Price Race to the Bottom

Government tenders prioritize lowest price (L1) over quality. TCS, with ₹12 lakh+ crore market cap and high overhead costs, cannot match the cut-rate pricing of smaller firms willing to operate on thin margins — or no margins at all, banking on upsells later.

Coempt bid ₹24.75 per answer sheet. TCS’s cost structure simply doesn’t allow that kind of pricing.

B. The Specs Get Written to Exclude

Here’s the structural manipulation: tender specifications are often drafted in ways that coincidentally match the capabilities of the preferred vendor. Requirements that TCS easily meets (CMMI Level 5 certification, 300+ DPI scanning, proven infrastructure) get relaxed after the first two rounds fail to produce the “right” winner.

CBSE dropped the CMMI Level 5 requirement in round 3. That’s not cost-cutting — that’s moving the goalposts.

C. Small Firms Are More “Flexible”

Government procurement officers often prefer vendors who don’t push back on arbitrary requirements. Large companies like TCS have legal and compliance teams that insist on proper processes. Smaller firms are more willing to work with looser arrangements.

This isn’t about efficiency. It’s about accountability — or the lack of it.

D. TCS Has Bigger Fish to Fry

From TCS’s perspective, exam tech is a small, messy business. Their iON platform does ~₹1,000 crore in revenue — but compared to their $7.5 billion quarterly revenue, it’s noise. The margins are thin, the scrutiny is political, and the upside is limited. They’ve been quietly scaling back.

E. No Quality Benchmarks in Tenders

Indian government tenders for exam technology do not require: - Independent security audits - Penetration testing results - Demonstrated uptime guarantees - Track record of handling similar-scale exams without failures - Vendor financial health checks

Without these, the tender becomes a price auction — and the cheapest bidder wins, regardless of whether they can actually deliver.

F. Cross-Party Protection

Vendors build relationships across political parties. When one party questions a contract, the vendor’s defenders point to contracts awarded by the other party. This creates political insulation — neither side wants to investigate too deeply because they both have fingerprints on the same vendors.

4. NEP 2020: The Coming Flood

Here’s where it gets personal for you. The National Education Policy (NEP) 2020 — the biggest overhaul of Indian education in 30+ years — is about to dramatically expand the role of exam-tech companies in your life.

What NEP 2020 Says About Exams

NEP 2020’s Section 4.4 mandates a shift to “competency-based assessment” — moving away from rote memorization to testing actual understanding. This sounds great in theory. In practice, it means:

  • More digital assessments — competency-based testing is harder to do on paper
  • More frequent evaluations — not just one final exam, but continuous assessment
  • More standardized benchmarks — PARAKH, the new national assessment regulator set up under NCERT, will set assessment standards for all 60+ school boards in India 6
  • More data collection — every assessment generates data that needs processing

What This Means for Exam-Tech Companies

NEP 2020 effectively creates a massive demand pipeline for exam-tech vendors:

  • State boards (30+) will need to digitize their assessment systems to meet PARAKH standards
  • CBSE will expand OSM to more subjects and classes
  • NTA is already expanding CBT to more exams — and NEET-UG moves to CBT from 2027 7
  • AI-driven proctoring, adaptive testing, automated marking — all of these are emerging requirements that only tech companies can provide

The NEET Timebomb

On May 29, 2026 — just two days before this article was written — the NTA told the Supreme Court that NEET-UG will move to computer-based test (CBT) mode from 2027. 8

NEET is India’s largest medical entrance exam. Over 24 lakh students took it in 2026. Moving 24 lakh students from pen-and-paper to CBT in one year is a logistical challenge of enormous scale.

Who will build and run that CBT infrastructure? The same opaque companies that botched CBSE OSM and SSC CBT. The tender process will follow the same pattern. The same small firms with political connections will bid low, win, and the system will fail.

NEET 2027 could be the next CBSE OSM — but at an even larger scale.

And the vendors serving this market have no regulatory oversight, no mandatory quality standards, and no transparency requirements.

5. The Reform Question

So what would actually fix this? Here’s what experts and student groups have been demanding:

Immediate Fixes

  1. Cancel the current contracts pending investigation. Both the CBSE OSM and SSC CBT contracts should be independently audited before any further exams are conducted through these vendors.

  2. Publish full tender documents. CBSE has not yet made the OSM tender results public — including qualifying amounts and bid details. This is mandatory for a government body. 9

  3. Mandatory vendor track record disclosure. If a company has been blacklisted, had contracts cancelled, or faced exam failures, this must be disclosed in the tender itself — not buried in fine print.

Structural Reforms

  1. Separate evaluation from delivery. The company that scans your answer sheets should not be the same company that evaluates them. Independent verification at every stage.

  2. Mandatory cooling-off periods. Government advisory board members (like professors serving on PSU boards and exam committees) should not be able to join companies bidding for government contracts within 3 years of their tenure ending.

  3. Independent security audits. Every exam-tech system must undergo annual penetration testing and security audits by independent agencies — results published publicly.

  4. Open-source alternatives. The government should invest in building open-source examination infrastructure — not locking millions of students’ futures into proprietary systems owned by private companies. If the system is open-source, anyone can audit it, fix bugs, and improve it.

  5. Financial health checks for vendors. Tenders must require vendors to demonstrate financial stability, minimum paid-up capital, and audited financials — not just a low bid price.

What’s Blocking Reform

The political economy of exam tech works against all of these fixes:

  • No political party wants to investigate too deeply because they all use the same vendors
  • The bureaucracy benefits from opaque procurement — fewer questions, fewer accountability trails
  • The vendors have no incentive to improve when failure has no consequences — they just blame “rumours” and sue critics
  • Students have no institutional power — you can tweet, protest, and file RTIs, but the system is designed to outlast your outrage

6. What You Can Do

You’re 18. You just had your exam results mangled by a system that wasn’t built for you — it was built for the convenience of the people who run it. Here’s what’s actually in your power:

  1. Document everything. If you spotted errors in your OSM evaluation, file a formal objection. Keep records. The Sarthak Sidhant blog — written by a Class 12 student who analysed the tender documents — forced national attention on the procurement process.

  2. File RTIs. Ask CBSE for: (a) full tender documents and bid details, (b) the quality audit reports on the OSM system, (c) the re-evaluation statistics — how many were changed, and why.

  3. Follow the money. The Coempt story isn’t about one company — it’s about a system. When the next exam scandal breaks (and it will), ask: who owns the vendor? Who sits on their board? Who advised them? Who invested in them?

  4. Support investigative journalism. The best coverage of this scandal has come from student bloggers and independent reporters, not mainstream media. Amplify their work.

  5. Demand open-source exam infrastructure. Your marks should not depend on proprietary software owned by a Hyderabad company with 5 directors and a history of failures. The government can build this in the open. Push for it.

TL;DR

  • Coempt (CBSE) and Eduquity (SSC) followed the same playbook: bid low after tender conditions were relaxed, win the contract, fail to deliver, get politically insulated.
  • TCS loses because government tenders prioritize price over quality, specs get rewritten to exclude them, and they have bigger businesses to run.
  • NEP 2020 and the NEET CBT transition are about to flood this broken market with even more demand — 30+ crore students dependent on the same opaque vendors.
  • Nobody in power has an incentive to fix this because all parties use the same vendors, and the bureaucracy benefits from opacity.
  • Your best weapon is documentation, RTIs, and public pressure. The Sarthak Sidhant blog proved that one student’s analysis can force national attention.

The scan errors and blurred marks on your answer sheets are symptoms. The disease is a system that allows opaque, under-qualified companies to become the nervous system of public examinations — with no consequences when they fail.

That the system permits this is the scandal. Your mangled marks are just the evidence.
