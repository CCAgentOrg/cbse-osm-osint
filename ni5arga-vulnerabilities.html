📖 The Short Version

Think of the CBSE marking system like a school's answer sheet storage room. ni5arga, a 19-year-old security researcher, discovered that:

🔑 The back door was wide open

  • The "master key" was left in the lock — visible to anyone who looked
  • The security guard checked IDs by asking you your name (not verifying against a list)
  • You could walk into any room without signing in
  • You could change anyone's password without knowing the old one
  • You could pick up any student's answer sheet from the storage room — no questions asked

He reported all of this to India's cybersecurity agency (CERT-In) on February 25, 2026. Three months later, after no meaningful fix, he went public.

📝 What's Already in His Blog (5 findings)

ni5arga documented his first 5 findings in a detailed blog post published May 22, 2026. Here's a simple summary:

The 5 Blogged Vulnerabilities

#What happenedIn plain English
1 Hardcoded master password in website code The system's master password was literally written in the website's source code — like taping your ATM PIN to the ATM machine
2 OTP sent back to the browser, not verified by server The login OTP was checked by your own browser, not the server. It's like a bouncer who asks "are you on the list?" and trusts whatever you say
3 No route guards — entire app accessible without login Every single page could be visited without logging in. The login screen was decorative
4 Change password without knowing old one Anyone could reset any evaluator's password without knowing or entering the current password
5 Systemic IDOR — ID manipulation across all APIs By changing a number in the web address, you could see any student's marks — like guessing someone's locker combination by trying 1, 2, 3

🆕 What Came After the Blog (3 new findings)

After publishing the blog, ni5arga kept finding more holes. These are the ones that hadn't been blogged as of May 31, 2026 — and they're arguably worse.

Finding 6: Took Over the Entire CBSE Production Server Critical

🟢 Reported to CERT-In, Education Ministry, CBSE · 🔴 Disclosed: May 29, 2026

ni5arga (along with @thetirthparmar) demonstrated that they had complete control of CBSE's actual, live production server — the one that was actively being used to grade Class 12 answer sheets.

🏠 Think of it like this

Imagine someone not just finding the back door to the school, but getting the master keys to every classroom, the principal's office, the records room, and the intercom system — and then playing music over the PA to prove it.

💥 What this means for students

  • Full CRUD access: They could Create, Read, Update, or Delete any data on the server — marks, evaluator assignments, student records, everything
  • Shell access: They could run any command on the server itself — install software, copy files, erase data
  • Proof: They played the "Bad Apple" video on the CBSE production website to demonstrate control
  • This was the live server — not a test environment, not a backup. The one grading 17.8 lakh students' actual answer sheets

Finding 7: Super Admin on a University Grading Portal Critical

🟢 Reported to CERT-In · 🔴 Disclosed: May 29, 2026

A separate OnMark subdomain — used by multiple universities for exam evaluation — was compromised, granting super admin access.

🏛️ Think of it like this

If Finding 6 was breaking into one school, this is breaking into the central office that manages grading for dozens of schools — and getting the principal's login.

💥 What they could do with super admin access

  • Grade any answer sheet — assign marks to any student at any university using the system
  • Edit any user's password — lock out evaluators or take over their accounts
  • View the full user list — see every evaluator, student, and administrator
  • Create new accounts — make fake evaluators, fake admins
  • Send mass SMS and email — to all evaluators and students, impersonating the university

Why this is bigger than CBSE: This proves the vulnerability isn't limited to CBSE. It's baked into the OnMark platform itself — used by multiple institutions across India. Tweet 1 · Tweet 2 · Tweet 3

Finding 8: Anyone Could Download Any Student's Answer Sheet Critical

🔴 Disclosed: May 31, 2026 · 🔴 476K+ views on X

CBSE's cloud storage (AWS S3 bucket) was left completely open — no password, no login, nothing. Anyone on the internet could browse, search, and download scanned answer sheets and question papers.

📁 Think of it like this

Imagine the school put every student's answer sheet in a cardboard box, left it on the sidewalk with a sign that says "free to take," and didn't even write a name on it. Anyone walking by — not just students of that school — could flip through and take whatever they wanted.

💥 Why this is the most damaging finding

  • Zero technical skill needed — no hacking, no coding, no tools. Just a web browser
  • Multiple institutions affected — not just CBSE. Several organizations shared the same open bucket
  • Full answer booklets — scanned images of students' handwritten answers, with identifying information
  • Question papers too — future exam content potentially exposed
  • Searchable and enumerable — the bucket allowed pagination, meaning someone could systematically download everything
"CBSE people didn't configure their AWS bucket properly and now we can paginate & enumerate all their media which has 2026 answersheets & question papers. ListObjectsV2 works without any auth and the bucket root is listable too — anyone on the internet can download any scanned booklet — across institutions." — @ni5arga, May 31, 2026

Finding 9: Payment Data Leak — 457,874 Student/Parent Records Exposed Critical

🔴 Disclosed: May 31, 2026 · Live at time of disclosure

An unauthenticated API endpoint on a CBSE-related payment portal was returning 457,874+ records of payment transaction data — including email addresses, phone numbers, payment IDs, order IDs, payment method, and status — to anyone who sent a request. No authentication required.

💳 Think of it like this

Imagine the school's fee collection office left a printout of every parent's payment receipt — with their name, email, phone number, and what they paid for — taped to the front window. Anyone walking past could photograph it. Now imagine that list has nearly half a million entries.

💥 What data was exposed

  • 457,874+ records — nearly half a million payment transactions
  • Email addresses — full addresses (gmail.com and others visible)
  • Phone numbers — in +91 (India) format
  • Payment IDs — unique transaction identifiers
  • Order IDs — order references
  • Payment method — netbanking visible in all sampled records
  • Payment status — 'failed' status visible across records

The data was extracted by calling an API endpoint that returned JSON, then displayed in the browser console via console.table(rows). The researcher did not need to bypass any login, use any exploit, or authenticate in any way — the endpoint simply served the data to anyone who asked.

"console.table(rows); Total visible records: 457874" — Browser DevTools screenshot from @ni5arga's disclosure tweet, May 31, 2026

⚠️ Why this is different from the other findings

  • This isn't about marks or answer sheets — it's about financial data
  • UPI/netbanking transactions mean bank-level PII is tied to these records
  • Failed payments could be retried by an attacker with the payment/order details
  • This data could enable spear-phishing — targeted emails/SMS posing as CBSE asking for "payment confirmation"
  • The scale (457K+ records) dwarfs individual student data exposure
